## P6-C01 — The condemned return alive

Source: [P6-C01](../source-map.md#p6-c01), blocks 1–43; [original HTML](../../../src/text/part0060.html).

- **Narrator limit:** Semipalov explicitly did not know all Gamov's plans. Only Gamov, Gonzalez, and Pustovoyt understood the conference's central display (4). Woodworth and Gonzalez prepared invitations; Gamov and Woodworth signed them (6). The Core is the governing inner group.
- **People and relations:** Knurka IX travels with Shirbai Shar, his minister, ambassador, and intelligence agent; Shar meets Anna Kursai. Great Lepin's ruler Lon Chudin and his brother, commander Kir Kirun, now openly quarrel. Queen Agnessa of Korina accompanies Nordag's President Franz Putrament and his daughter Luiza. Lyudmila Miloshevskaya escorts mutually hostile Vilkomir Torba and Ponsiy Markvard. Pimen Georgiu and Konstantin Fagusta watch each other. Amentola's companions are Norma Friz and Radon Torkin, despite their earlier hostile public statements (7–8). These descriptions are narrator reports, not proof that each hostility has ended.
- **Observed reversal of reported deaths:** Torkin's unnamed daughter returns alive (19–28). Nordag's condemned captured officers return, led by army commander Sumo Pariona and his chief of staff Kinza Vardant, both lieutenant generals (31). This chapter's original HTML says **Suma** in a nominative construction; the earlier self-introduction at P2-C09 block 15 says **Sumo**. Treat as one person with a source spelling variation, not a case-form distinction or a second general. Gonzalez and Pustovoyt reveal friendly coordination (33). Hundreds of people previously announced executed appear (35). Semipalov recalls his own staged execution and infers an established technique (35).
- **Limit on reversal:** Semipalov directly distinguishes real battlefield deaths from this protected subset of a subset of prisoners (35). Do not convert the revelation into “nobody was killed.” Gamov's tears and physical weakness are witnessed and broadcast the next day (36–39).
- **Place and world:** Conference in Adan, in the hall where Gamov recently suffered a heart attack. Roughly three hundred initial guests stand before the staged entrance; natural weather occurs because Gamov ordered Shtupa to use no meteorological generator (10–15).
- **Reconciliation dependencies:** Locate prior execution reports for Torkin's daughter, Pariona, Vardant, and other named captives; locate Semipalov's mock execution and his reproach to Pustovoyt about good conditions “after execution.” The ending identifies intentional concealment, not a confirmed authorial contradiction (29–35). The narrator's claim that Torkin's interruption was unplanned is explicitly his belief (20).
- **Chapter review:** The public resurrection is factual survival, not supernatural revival. Named rescued officers remain distinct from the many unnamed survivors. No confirmed continuity error established within this chapter.

## P6-C02 — The cost of a useful deception

Source: [P6-C02](../source-map.md#p6-c02), blocks 1–61; [original HTML](../../../src/text/part0061.html).

- **Secret's authorship and purpose:** Gamov claims he devised the false-execution system; Gonzalez and Pustovoyt agreed (6). Three Core members knew; seven did not (6–8). Gamov says Semipalov's inability to hide his feelings made his sincere hatred of Gonzalez a valuable political instrument, so keeping the secret from him required excluding the other six (15–24). This is Gamov's explanation, not access to every participant's private thought.
- **Earlier events reinterpreted:** Gamov says Semipalov almost believed his own deception of spy Jean Voityuk (16–18). He says he subtly prompted Semipalov's false confession of betrayal; Semipalov withheld the mock-execution plan from Elena to protect its credibility (22). Reconcile each claim with the earlier scene, including Elena's real distress.
- **Mercy and culpability:** Gonzalez reports wider releases elsewhere (29). He admits real killings by the Ministry of Terror and Black Court; “several hundred” saved people do not remove his guilt (33). He accepts that one innocent killing could morally outweigh hundreds saved, while questioning how guilt should be judged (35–41). Do not equate his reported total with the hall's attendance or the total number executed. Semipalov leaves uncertain whether Gonzalez changed or his own perception changed (40).
- **People and fates:** Philosopher Orest Biber and writer Arnold Falk were captured in the collapse of Mart Tronshke's army. Falk worked at an energy-water factory, then returned home after release to plan an antiwar novel (43–48). General Arman Pliss says retirement will let him study Biber (49–50); his claim of lifelong devotion to peace is ironic character testimony, not settled history.
- **Institutional proposal:** Core accepts Biber's scheme: Gamov as chief world president; council of national presidents including monarchs; perpetual peace; dissolved armies; destroyed weapons except police requirements; abolition of borders, customs, visas, passports; common currency; free residence (52–53). These are adopted proposals here, not completed implementation. Biber will give the detailed conference report; Gamov the general speech (58–61).
- **Chapter review:** C01's rescue revelation does not absolve terror. This chapter expressly confirms the distinction. All ten Core members are implied by three plus seven; membership identities need earlier-part reconciliation. No new confirmed contradiction.

## P6-C03 — The victor demands a trial

Source: [P6-C03](../source-map.md#p6-c03), blocks 1–96; [original HTML](../../../src/text/part0062.html).

- **Settlement and chronology:** Gamov's address receives a hymn; Biber speaks for three hours. Presidents and monarchs accept world union under Gamov, with minor reservations. They leave to reorganize national authority. Adan becomes temporary world capital (4–6). Unlike C02's proposals, these conference approvals and departures are narrated as completed.
- **Narrative frame:** Semipalov consciously shifts from international history to the coming crisis of his own identity and responsibility (2). His omission of detailed constitutional proceedings is stated, not a missing causal bridge to invent.
- **Gamov's demand:** As victorious leader, Gamov wants public trial of the good and evil of his conduct. He rejects immunity for winners, wants assessment rather than punishment for its own sake, and claims sole responsibility for wrongdoing (11–29). Semipalov contests that exclusion of the collaborators who implemented policy (27–30).
- **Prior planning revealed:** Gonzalez says Gamov proposed the future trial when appointing him Minister of Terror (35–40). Gamov says he discussed courtroom roles with the editors when appointing them to opposing papers (45–53). Loyalist Pimen Georgiu is to prosecute; critic Konstantin Fagusta to defend. This later testimony reinterprets earlier appointments; match it against their original scenes during global reconciliation. Others deny prior knowledge (57–58), even though C03 block 9 describes Pustovoyt as apparently knowing the immediate meeting's purpose. Those are different claims, not a proved contradiction.
- **Defendants and procedural uncertainty:** Gonzalez asks to be both judge and defendant, as Gamov's “left hand” and as a judge whose decisions always win. Semipalov, the “right hand,” agrees to stand trial too (61–85). Gonzalez must devise the procedure; no independent tribunal structure is yet supplied. Gamov's objection to ridicule is Semipalov's interpretation (86).
- **Chapter review:** Preserve the unusual judge/defendant combination as an explicit issue in the narrative. Do not normalize it into ordinary legal procedure or treat Semipalov's accusation of madness as a diagnosis. No confirmed continuity error.

## P6-C04 — Semipalov reviews the road to victory

Source: [P6-C04](../source-map.md#p6-c04), blocks 1–50; [original HTML](../../../src/text/part0063.html). This file continues into C05 at its separate chapter anchor.

- **Narrator's revised model:** Semipalov interprets Gamov's apparent impulses as calculated deviations toward a known goal, whose full disclosure could have alienated his helpers (4–7). This is retrospective interpretation, not proof that every event was planned.
- **Earlier-part reconciliation dependencies:** Street rescue and near-biting of the knife attacker; later proclamation of “fierce” rule, less than a year after that encounter; humiliation of captive Birker Shtok; first distribution of captured money to soldiers; later institutional payment for valor; the engineered circumstances and technique of Semipalov's false execution (8). Keep each original observation distinct from the narrator's new claim of systematic policy. His hypothetical refusal to cooperate is counterfactual.
- **Food appeal:** Semipalov recalls the leadership's refusal to feed enemies and Gamov's successful direct appeal linking public food sacrifice to his own health (9). He believes Gamov would really have died had the answer been no. This belief does not prove a medical mechanism or prove the illness was feigned. Reconcile the original appeal and medical evidence.
- **Political interpretation:** Semipalov says the war is won and the states' union removes the basis of interstate hostility (11). This is his present claim about achieved peace, not proof that conflict has become impossible.
- **Elena and Semipalov:** Elena reads the secret trial commitment as Gamov's means of accepting future accountability for unconventional methods (34–43). She sees acquittal or punishment as cleansing. Semipalov recognizes that her admiration of Gamov is not romantic love, yet remains hurt by it (39). He raises actual execution as a possible outcome and refuses to predict Gonzalez's self-sentencing (44–49).
- **Chapter review:** Distinguish Elena's interpretation from Gamov's stated purpose in C03. Both may be compatible, but the narrator has not confirmed all her reasoning. The chapter ends with Elena shaking her head. No confirmed continuity error.

Day one is procedural; day two Georgiu's indictment; later sittings continue the exchange (6–7,18,46).

Allegations include humiliation in manure, new punishment for already adjudicated crimes, cancellation of death-sentence commutations, northern labor, and collective punishment of relatives and beneficiaries (11–12). His exiled young-woman example remains a prosecutor's account pending response.

His family was exiled; property confiscated; Semipalov approved the sentence (13). Later responses may supply omitted context. Do not silently accept or reject the prosecutor's framing.
- **Narrator admission:** Semipalov calls many charges indisputable, naming execution of Maravan-khor and his circle in Konduk, repression there, and retaliatory torture/execution by freed prisoners against former guards (14). Reconcile exact earlier incidents and who ordered each act.
- **Institutional charges:** Rewards for killings and family death compensation could incentivize death, Georgiu argues; he explicitly does not accuse all families of this motive (15). He attacks Sacred Terror and the Black Court Joint-Stock Company for rewarding foreign violence prohibited at home (16). Distinguish risk argument from demonstrated individual behavior.

Semipalov recalls paper-bearing visits but had assumed guidance, not authorship (37). Fagusta's exceptional shock is observed; its cause remains unknown (38–46). Earlier newspaper positions need reassessment as publication positions, not necessarily editor beliefs.

## P6-C07 — Gamov wrote both sides of the dispute

Source: [P6-C07](../source-map.md#p6-c07), blocks 1–65; [original HTML](../../../src/text/part0065.html).

- **Defense's positive case:** Fagusta cites rescue of enemy children from water allergy, food aid to enemies, and concealed survival of condemned people (8). These repeat earlier events but constitute the defense's moral synthesis. Omar Isiro organizes full stereo coverage (8).
- **Prisoner-food dispute:** Georgiu alleges near-halving of normal rations, then taking more than half the food sent by Cortezia. Fagusta confirms the harsh surface but adds family supply and camp visits, hospital/child-welfare redistribution, and inspection access for Norma Friz's Relief Administration (10–14). Verify the original percentages, recipients, and permissions against prior parts; do not treat moral disagreement as a numerical contradiction.
- **Major revelation:** Fagusta says Gamov wrote the *Tribune*'s hostile unsigned editorials while also writing the *Herald*'s praise (20–29). Fagusta had supported the policy as a whole. Each editor was unaware of the other's secret arrangement. This resolves his C06 shock. Gonzalez also did not know (32,46); Semipalov's sense of intimate knowledge is again overturned (30–31).
- **Reported origin and motives:** Fagusta recounts a secret overnight meeting: Gamov proposed controlled criticism to channel dissatisfaction and forestall an internal opposition front supported by Cortezia; he also wanted to examine resistance generated by policy (35–43). Gamov provided the first article that day. These motives are Gamov's words as reported by Fagusta, not independent proof of every effect on readers.
- **Private outcome:** Gamov plans an accusatory speech defending the trial's necessity. Semipalov promises a defense that will accuse Gamov (53–65). Serbin's hope that Semipalov will save Gamov is the narrator's reading of a glance, not explicit dialogue (48).
- **Chronology tension P6-T01:** C06 says day one was procedural and day two the prosecution (6–7), then closes that sitting and assigns Fagusta's response to the next day (18). After the authorship exchange, C06 closes again until the following day (46). C07 describes an intervening night and a new day (2,7), but closes it as the “third day” (47). The narrated sequence appears to imply a fourth day. Retain as a **numerical/chronological tension**, pending full-sequence review; do not silently repair the ordinal.
- **Chapter review:** The dual newspaper authorship is intentional concealed agency, not a contradiction between Gamov's published opinions. The court-day count is a separate possible continuity error.

## P6-C08 — Voityuk's accusation and the shadows of good

Source: [P6-C08](../source-map.md#p6-c08), blocks 1–61; [original HTML](../../../src/text/part0066.html).

- **Voityuk's fate:** Jean Voityuk survives. Semipalov had inferred death from Prishchepa's failure to locate him; the inference was wrong (3–7). His poor physical state is observed. His account says he hid in slums after defeat (23). Reconcile earlier disappearance, not as resurrection or established prior death.
- **Identity claim:** Voityuk says he was born in Latania, is half Latanian, and has a Cortezian father and grandfather. He chose unpaid espionage for Cortezia as the homeland of his allegiance (19–21). Those claims distinguish his chosen loyalty from Semipalov's legal/political conception of treason. His unnamed wife is recalled as having been in Gonzalez's power (6); this chapter does not establish her present fate.
- **Accusation one:** Semipalov gave a real secret invasion threat against Nordag to Voityuk, inducing Putrament's withdrawal from Zabon. Voityuk calls disclosure treason even though it served a successful deception; his failed-trick scenario is counterfactual (26–29). Reconcile the prior operational plan and what exactly was disclosed.
- **Accusation two:** Voityuk claims humanitarian sacrifice was calculated for eventual Latanian benefit and therefore betrayed public professions of altruism (31–35). It is an argumentative motive claim, not proof that aid was unreal or that beneficiaries did not benefit.
- **Semipalov's defense:** Good can produce unavoidable harm; victory alone is not proof of goodness; justice must weigh overall benefit and injury (44–48). The water-allergy/drought/crop-loss/hunger chain is his concrete example (45). His “cutters of shadows” argument attacks the trial's premise. He does not offer a law that all harms are justified automatically.
- **Outcome:** Gamov says the speech changes his perspective; Gonzalez announces a rebuttal for tomorrow (50–56). Elena's response is admiration, not an independent verdict (59–61). Defenders and accusers may remain in court; ordinary spectators cannot (41).
- **P6-T01 update:** Narrator calls this the fourth trial day (2), consistent with C07's stated third day, but the extra adjournment in C06 remains unexplained.
- **Chapter review:** Survival resolves an inference of death, not a confirmed contradiction. Whether calculated help is morally compromised remains an explicit philosophical dispute.

## P6-C09 — Gonzalez asks for his own death

Source: [P6-C09](../source-map.md#p6-c09), blocks 1–26; [original HTML](../../../src/text/part0067.html). C10 begins later in the same source file.

- **Earlier loyalties revealed:** Gonzalez says he and Albert Peano recognized Gamov and Semipalov as their best hope during encirclement by Cortezia/Roder and the defecting Patinians. Peano, rather than Gamov's rhetoric, overcame Gonzalez's resistance to paying soldiers (6–7).
- **Retrospective rank mismatch R6-01:** Gonzalez recalls Gamov as lieutenant colonel and Semipalov as major during that encirclement, before the Parp raid (6–7). [P1-C03](../source-map.md#p1-c03) block 3 explicitly gives Gamov a direct promotion from major to colonel three days before the Barta action; the Patina defection and encirclement news follow in blocks 42–68, and the raid follows in [P1-C05](../source-map.md#p1-c05). The recalled lieutenant-colonel rank does not match that sequence. Classify as a retrospective rank mismatch and possible source slip; do not invent an intermediate promotion or demotion.
- **Name variation R6-02:** Block 7 names the raid's opponent Peter **Porp**, whereas [P1-C05](../source-map.md#p1-c05) names Peter **Parp**. The matching raid and role identify the same officer. Preserve Parp as the earlier canonical name and record Porp as a source spelling variation, not another person or a Russian case form.
- **Mordasov's death:** Gonzalez says executing government envoy Danilo Mordasov was his first actual attack with a weapon. He had avoided even target practice. He and Peano initially thought Gamov assigned the act to bind Gonzalez through shared wrongdoing (7–8). This is a real killing in his confession, not one of the later mock executions. Verify original order, weapon sequence, and rank.
- **Terror/Mercy arrangement:** Gamov proposed the ministry the day after taking power, arguing that Gonzalez's aversion to cruelty qualified him. After a sleepless night, Gonzalez met Pustovoyt at noon. Real sentences required absence of substantial mercy objections; political necessity plus a valid objection led to simulation. Pustovoyt organized the secret engineering group and shelters until victory (9–11). Earlier public hostility between the ministers is reinterpreted as concealment.
- **Self-sentence logic:** Gonzalez privately accepted death for himself when he first condemned another. He later rejected one life as an adequate balance for hundreds taken. He now demands his own execution but says formal sentencing is still future (10–12). Semipalov fears the implication for all three defendants (17); that is not yet a sentence.
- **Biber's defense:** Former critic from Klur, later prisoner, argues that dynamic historical results outweigh isolated harms. He cites the water-aircraft division's mutiny and Gamov's praise of officers whose trainees surpassed them, and the replacement of interstate hostility with mutual aid (19–26). Link the original mutiny during global reconciliation.
- **Chronology tension P6-T01, stronger evidence:** C09 opens the “fourth day” (2), although C08 opened the fourth day (2), ended with Gonzalez's promise to rebut “tomorrow” (56), and then records Semipalov's evening at home (59–61). This is an explicit repeated ordinal across a narrated night, in addition to the C06/C07 counting problem. Preserve as a source chronology inconsistency unless later evidence resolves it.
- **Numerical tension P6-T02:** C02 block 33 speaks of “several hundred” prevented executions; C09 block 11 says mock-execution operations had already occurred “thousandfold” before Semipalov's case. The latter may be rhetorical emphasis rather than a census. Record conflicting scale language; do not assign a fabricated exact total.
- **Chapter review:** Gonzalez's appearance/character mismatch is resolved for Semipalov through disclosure, not bodily transformation. The claim that all real deaths were avoided is expressly false. Trial-day, execution-scale, retrospective rank, and name-spelling issues remain separately classified.

## P6-C11 — The individual victim and the unknown origin

Source: [P6-C11](../source-map.md#p6-c11), blocks 1–144; [original HTML](../../../src/text/part0068.html). Catalog consulted: [P4-C02](part-4-ledger.md#p4-c02) and [P4-C03](part-4-ledger.md#p4-c03) for the two physicists and their qualified origin claims; P3-C08 for Tarkhun-khor's identity.

- **Gamov's moral argument:** General benefit and individual suffering cannot simply share a scale. An individual death destroys that person's whole experienced world. Conscience generates compassion and nontransferable responsibility (7–15). Gamov identifies himself as chiefly responsible for an unnamed girl's death in an enemy air raid because its ultimate political target was him, and demands death (15). This is moral self-accusation, not a claim that he personally dropped the bomb.
- **Concrete earlier-event evidence:** Girl about six to eight; raid after defeats of Ferdinand Vaksel and Mart Tronshke; filmed dead with arms raised. Retaliation killed pilots at airfields or, another week later, dropped living captives over their own cities (11–15). Verify the original raid/reprisals, dates, and victims against earlier parts. Do not merge this girl with the child seen in the other world's nuclear attack (121).
- **Tarkhun-khor:** Konduk's high priest, claimed deputy/descendant of Mamun; says he is the 74th living embodiment but only the 69th biological-generation descendant (19,25–28). Semipalov recalls that Isiro spared him and other clergy after the punishment of Konduk, but Gamov's prior orchestration is only a suspicion contradicted by his visible surprise (20). Tarkhun says he witnessed Maravan-khor's execution and considered it deserved (59).
- **Gamov's memory and rescue:** No known parents, birthplace, or memories before rescue near Sorbas by observatory staff. He recalls being carried, placed on a cart, and treated by a doctor. The caravan explanation was rescuers' conjecture after a severe sandstorm, not his remembered experience (45–68). He never excavated for parental remains. Compare earlier accounts without retroactively upgrading the conjecture into fact.
- **Priest's investigation and inference:** Reported trade-record search in both countries and subsurface instrument survey found no caravan; these are Tarkhun's testimony. He infers interworld arrival, admits absence of the hard proof Gamov demands, and offers Mamun's songs instead (78–108). Court rejects their interpretation as conclusive legal evidence (126–127).
- **Songs and world history:** First song depicts a homeland divided by languages; second describes divine/cosmic upheaval and transfer of a people; third predicts unification of their now single-language descendants by a future leader (108–119). Semipalov treats messianic prophecy as poetic hope, but sees possible remembered knowledge of another world (120–122). These are not yet narrator-certified historical events in every detail.
- **Scientific dependency:** Semipalov recalls actual instrument viewing with nuclear physicist Berthold Shvurts and chronophysicist Berthold Kozyura, and the nuclear destruction of a different child (121). He and Pavel had not informed Gamov because the work was unfinished. He now calls the physicists (133–136). [P4-C02–C03](part-4-ledger.md#p4-c02) preserve the earlier observed images versus Kozyura's inferred origin.
- **Chronological plausibility P6-T03:** Mamun's predictions are called five centuries old (117,136), while Tarkhun claims the 69th generation from him (28). Ordinary consecutive parent-child generations over that interval would average about seven years. The source distinguishes incarnations from generations but gives no calendar or genealogy explanation. Retain a numerical/chronological tension pending global review; do not invent a resolution or call the incarnation count itself an error.
- **Chapter review:** Gamov's origin remains unresolved here. The neighboring world's existence has earlier observational support; identifying Gamov as its emigrant still rests on inference. Conscience and shame are explicitly distinguished by Semipalov (121).

## P6-C12 — Sentence, intervention, and the desire to disappear

Source: [P6-C12](../source-map.md#p6-c12), blocks 1–74; [original HTML](../../../src/text/part0069.html).

- **Actual verdict:** Black Court sentences Alexei Gamov, his deputy/War Minister Andrei Semipalov, and Terror Minister/chairman Arkady Gonzalez to death, final and unappealable under its rules (3–5). Unlike earlier fears, this sentence is now an observed event.
- **Intervention executed:** Isiro's cameras transmit Semipalov's “Time!” signal. Colonel Korney Kaplin leads water-aircraft troops; Ivan Kordobin and Jean Vilta surround Gonzalez, Sergei Skripnik and Alfred Palman surround Gamov. Gonzalez throws three soldiers down, but Vilta holds on long enough for the others to restrain him (6–14). Court guards are arrested and removed; Gamov and Gonzalez are placed in custody (11,36). Semipalov remains free and assumes governmental authority (29).
- **Referendum announced:** Semipalov suspends enforcement without claiming to annul the lawful court's decision. Preparation starts tomorrow; the sole question is whether the sentence on the three is just. The world electorate is his proposed higher court (27–35). No vote or result occurs here.
- **Political reactions:** Core members enter except Pavel, who is fetching the physicists. Woodworth disapproves of the irregularity; Bar, Shtupa, Pustovoyt, Peano, and Isiro show approval (22,36–38). Gamov's comparison to Marutzian's overthrow is explicit; Semipalov says the purpose is preservation of Gamov's authority (19–20).
- **Gamov's stated motive:** Guarded in his own rooms, he expects the vote to overturn death but is troubled by that outcome. He wants an effective dramatic ending and a lasting philosophical question, rather than routine peaceful administration (59–68). This supplements, without automatically disproving, C11's conscience argument.

## P6-C13 — A living departure replaces martyrdom

Religious deference is a narrator explanation for voting behavior, not a counted demographic audit.

This is inconsistent presentation but not two equally firm counts: uncertainty precedes a categorical assertion. Preserve lack of a reliable total; do not manufacture percentages.

Army demobilization and conversion of military factories are now underway (22), resolving C10's continued existence of armed formations after C02's disarmament proposal.

- **Chapter review:** Actual abolition and release replace earlier intentions. Gamov's “bodilessness” is a metaphor for symbolic public identity, not a supernatural physical change (31–36).

## P6-C15 — The peace-maker faces another divided world

Source: [P6-C15](../source-map.md#p6-c15), blocks 1–64; [original HTML](../../../src/text/part0071.html). Blocks 60 and 62 are empty source tags; 63–64 are the authorial date/place colophon, not story chronology.

- **Arrival and place:** Semipalov wakes in a military medical room. Later he learns he is confined on floor 128 of a military center (2–9,38). Ten major international languages suffice for broad communication; educated people commonly know their own and two international languages (3,11–15). Four thousand is archive coverage, explicitly not every language ever spoken.

He has no subsequent news of Gamov, the Core, Elena, or Pavel. His prediction that Elena will eventually love Pavel rests on regret and hope. Pavel's lifelong love and celibate devotion are Semipalov's retrospective assertions (34), not evidence of a later marriage.
- **Rescue and time:** Semipalov says cabin construction took decades and recalls Kozyura's first-visit estimate: one homeland year approximately equals twenty other-world years (35). He therefore doubts rescue within his useful lifetime. No second cabin, return journey, or death occurs. The independently reread first laboratory scenes, P4-C02–C03, contain no such rate: P4-C02 block 70 discusses a century of superimposed screen images and finer viewing resolution, not relative clock speed. Natural crossing and regular transport are research aims, not proven mechanisms (37). This lends context to Mamun's songs without independently proving Gamov's birthplace.

Segovia confirms deliberate isolation to protect a state monopoly on energy-water, weather control, vehicles, and weapons, whose adoption could bring world supremacy (42). Without a weather generator, the accumulator cannot make rain (53–58). He resumes the posture of a meeting chair and asks who wants to speak (59–61).
- **Ending boundary:** Actual transfer and survival are confirmed by narrator experience. Diplomatic success, renewed war, technical transfer, eventual rescue, Gamov's return to office, and lasting peace at home are **not shown**. The last action begins a meeting; it does not resolve it. Colophon reads 1986–1987, Kaliningrad–Komarovo (63–64).
- **Chapter review:** Keep voluntary Gamov departure separate from forced Semipalov displacement; narrator authority ends at the world boundary for later homeland events. His final determination repeats the book's tension between collective peace, personal loss, ambition, and moral responsibility without furnishing a final verdict.
