Dictator · Sergei Snegov

A world at war. A promise of peace.

Introduction

Sergei Snegov's Dictator is a political science-fiction novel about the effort to end war through power, persuasion, terror, and mercy. This English account follows the Russian EPUB supplied in this repository: six parts and all 73 chapters, in their original order. The source map identifies their boundaries, including chapters that share an HTML file or continue into another. Full spoilers follow, including the final revelations and the ending.

The story begins in a world of rival states whose leaders turn a small border dispute into a widening war. Latania faces the military and economic strength of Cortezia, while smaller countries bargain, change sides, and pursue their own interests. Adan, the Latanian capital, and the threatened city of Zabon become centers of political and military action. The places catalog traces these relationships. Technology brings the war into fields, homes, and hospitals: concentrated energy-water powers machines and weapons, while weather generators alter rainfall and unleash destructive storms, with imperfect control. Food production and civilian survival are therefore central to strategy. The world reference explains these technologies and the institutions built around them.

At the center stands Alexei Gamov, an astrophysicist whose force of personality and military invention carry him toward dictatorship. He argues that peace requires an attack on the prestige, rewards, and habits that make war acceptable. His methods include payments for battlefield success, humiliating punishments, public threats, international courts, and carefully managed broadcasts. He also demands mercy toward enemies and costly aid for their children and hungry populations. These policies repeatedly pull against one another. The novel tests the relationship between effective action and moral responsibility, including the harm done by those who claim to act for humanity.

The account comes from Andrei Semipalov, an engineer who becomes a commander, Gamov's deputy, and an important maker of policy. He is an active participant with ambitions and loyalties of his own. He admires Gamov, protests against him, carries out decisions he opposes, and sometimes discovers that his sincere reactions have served a plan concealed from him. His retrospective narration can explain what he learned later while preserving the fear or certainty he felt at the time. A reported execution, a public declaration, or an accusation must therefore be read with attention to who knows what. Some apparent reversals reveal deception or mistaken belief; others remain unexplained.

Private relationships give these political choices their human cost. Semipalov's marriage to Elena suffers as public duty absorbs his life. Friendship becomes command, trust becomes an instrument of intelligence work, and compassion can become a means of securing obedience. The contrast between Gonzalez's role in terror and Pustovoyt's role in mercy is especially important, but their public reputations do not disclose everything they do. The character catalog follows changing offices, relationships, and identities. Speeches about conscience, patriotism, religion, and sacrifice remain arguments made by particular characters. Their force, and even their practical success, do not make every premise an established fact or a direct statement of the author's position.

The final chapter also explains the narrative frame. After an involuntary journey to another world, Semipalov records the history of his life with Gamov for the authorities holding him under military supervision. They see strategic value in his society's inventions. His effort to explain a struggle for peace may thus supply another divided world with means of domination. The novel ends as he opens a meeting with its government; the result of that meeting is not shown. His later thoughts about people left behind remain hopes or conjectures. Connections between the worlds, and claims about Gamov's origin, require the same distinction between observation, testimony, and interpretation.
